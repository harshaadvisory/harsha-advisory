Introduction

The spa business is often seen as a high-margin opportunity. A net margin of 40% or even higher sometimes comes up in conversations about this business, and the logic sounds reasonable: a single massage session sells for hundreds of thousands of rupiah, while the materials (oil, towels, tea) look inexpensive.

But there is an important difference between margin per session and business margin. And data from real spa operators shows that the difference is far wider than is often assumed.

Take Siam Wellness Group, the largest spa operator in Thailand, which is listed on the stock exchange and runs the Let's Relax and RarinJinda chains with 91 branches. In 2025, the company's net profit margin was not 40%, but around 11.46% of total revenue. And even that figure was down almost 39% from the previous year. If a spa operator of this calibre, with corporate scale and efficiency, posts margins only in the low teens, where does the 40% figure come from?

This article breaks down the cost structure of a spa business line by line, using real data from a listed operator, explains why the 40% figure should be read with caution, and highlights the factors that actually determine whether a spa's margin is healthy. From our experience supporting many kinds of companies, the businesses that look most luxurious are often the ones whose cost structure demands the most discipline.

Note: some of the comparison data comes from overseas markets and serves as a reference, not as a fixed Indonesian standard. The figures in the case example are illustrative.

Evidence from real data: net margin of around 11%, not 40%

Rather than theorise, let us look at real figures from Siam Wellness Group's official financial statements for 2025.

The company reported revenue from sales and services of around 1,622.8 million baht. Of that, the cost of services (COGS) took 73.48%, leaving a gross profit of only 28%. Note that even at the gross level, before marketing and administrative expenses, more than 70% of revenue was already used up. This is the first sign that the assumption that "spa costs are small" needs to be revisited.

After deducting selling and administrative expenses of around 12%, as well as interest and tax, net profit came to 188.7 million baht. Against revenue from sales and services, this equals 11.63%. Against the company's total revenue (including other income), the ratio is 11.46%. On either basis, the figure is in the low teens, far from 40%. And it was down 38.96% from the previous year.

Why such a sharp fall? The company's report gives two reasons that are highly relevant to our context. First, a decline in tourist numbers, particularly Chinese visitors, who make up its main customer base. Second, higher fixed costs from opening new branches: rent, utilities, maintenance and depreciation. The company opened 14 new branches that year, and the fixed costs of that expansion squeezed its margin.

Two immediate lessons emerge. The Siam Wellness data shows that even a large-scale spa operator, with corporate efficiency, can post a net margin in the low teens, not 40%. This shows that the 40% figure should be read with caution. And that margin, whatever its level, is highly sensitive to two things: customer flow and the burden of fixed costs. We will look at both in more depth.

Why the "40% margin" figure should be read with caution

If a listed operator as large as Siam Wellness posts margins in the low teens, where does the 40% figure that sometimes circulates come from? There are several explanations, and understanding them matters so that you do not miscalculate.

Margin per session is mistaken for business margin. A single treatment can indeed have a high gross margin if you count only consumables. But margin per session is not business margin. Between the two sit therapist wages, rent, utilities, marketing and other fixed costs that do not show up in a per-session calculation.

The 40% figure usually belongs to a different business model. High net margins in the wellness industry generally come from luxury resorts or med-spas whose costs are subsidised by a parent business (a hotel or clinic), or from spas that do not fully count the owner's salary. For a standalone day spa, this figure is rarely realistic.

The owner's salary is often not counted. This is a classic mistake. Many small owner-run spas look high-margin because the owner's time and effort are not included as a cost. Once a fair salary for the owner is counted, the real margin shrinks dramatically.

Empty beds are not counted as a loss. Optimistic margin calculations often assume the spa is always full. In reality, realistic treatment room utilisation rarely comes close to 100%, and every hour a bed sits empty is a fixed cost running with no revenue.

So when you see a promise of a 40% margin, the first question worth asking is: is that margin per session or business margin, and have all costs (including the owner's salary and empty beds) been counted?

Breaking down the spa cost structure

Let us break down where a spa's revenue goes, line by line. This structure has a distinctive character that sets it apart from other businesses such as cafes.

Labour (therapists): the largest line item. This is the defining feature of the spa business. Unlike a cafe, where ingredients dominate, a spa sells human services, so therapist wages become one of the biggest costs. Siam Wellness's own report names therapist wages as the main variable cost component, and flags the shortage of skilled therapists as a key operational risk. A number of international wellness industry benchmarks also place labour cost as the largest component, although the ranges differ by business model and market. The pay structure is also distinctive: it often combines base salary, commission per treatment and tips. Add BPJS contributions (Indonesia's state health and employment social security programmes) and allowances, and the real cost per therapist is higher than base salary alone.

Rent: heavy, and heavier still in premium locations. A spa needs a space that is comfortable, quiet and often in a strategic location, all of which is expensive. How heavy the rent burden is can be seen in Siam Wellness's balance sheet: right-of-use assets (which reflect lease commitments) reached 33% of total assets. Rent is not a small cost in the spa business, but one of the main determinants of its viability.

Utilities and supplies: higher than expected. A spa uses a lot of hot water, electricity for heating and cooling, intensive towel laundry, and consumables such as oils and care products. All of these keep running even when occupancy is low.

Marketing and administration. To attract and retain customers, especially in tourism-dependent markets, marketing costs become significant. At Siam Wellness, selling and administrative expenses came to around 12 to 14% of revenue.

When all these line items are added up, it is clear why a 28% gross margin can shrink to a net margin in the low teens. There is no single giant cost, but an accumulation of expenses that are each substantial.

The factors that really determine spa margins

Beyond the cost line items, there are several factors that separate a healthy spa from a dying one. These are what business owners should pay attention to.

Treatment room utilisation. This is the most decisive metric in the spa business, and the one most often ignored. A spa has high fixed costs (rent, wages, utilities) that run continuously. If treatment rooms are often empty, those fixed costs keep running without generating revenue. Raising utilisation from, say, 35% to 50% often has a far bigger impact on margin than cutting other costs, because that additional revenue goes almost entirely towards covering costs that are already being incurred.

Dependence on tourists. This is the sharpest lesson from Siam Wellness. Its profit fell 39% mainly because of the drop in Chinese tourists. This is a direct warning for spas in tourist areas: revenue that depends on one tourist segment is very fragile. When tourist flows change, margins can collapse quickly, while fixed costs such as rent and wages do not fall with them.

Channel mix: direct customers vs agents and platforms. Customers who come through travel agents, apps or booking platforms usually come with commissions that erode margin. A spa that can build a base of direct, repeat customers has a healthier margin structure than one that relies on high-commission channels.

The cost of expansion. Siam Wellness shows that opening new branches actually squeezes margins in the short term, because a new branch's fixed costs (rent, renovation, wages, depreciation) arrive before its revenue matures. Expanding too fast without proper calculation can drag down the margin of the entire business.

The Bali context: opportunity and vulnerability

For spa businesses in Bali, the findings above are highly relevant and need to be read honestly.

Bali is a large spa market, supported by strong tourist flows. That is a real opportunity. But the lesson from Siam Wellness applies directly: dependence on tourists is a double-edged sword. Spas in tourist zones enjoy high demand in peak periods, but face major risk when tourist flows change, whether because of seasons, global conditions or a shift in where visitors come from.

On the cost side, a spa's two largest line items also press harder in Bali. Rent in premium tourist areas is high and becomes an inflexible fixed cost. Meanwhile, therapist wages follow the applicable minimum wage, which in regencies such as Badung is among the highest in Bali. The combination of tourist-zone rent and labour costs means the cost structure of a spa in Bali tends to be heavy.

The implication is that a spa in Bali that wants to be financially healthy cannot rely on busy tourist seasons alone. It needs to maintain utilisation, build repeat customers (including local and expatriate markets that are not seasonal), and manage rent and labour costs with discipline.

Case illustration: a spa that is busy but runs on thin margins

This case example is illustrative and does not represent any specific client engagement. Figures are simplified.

Imagine a day spa in a tourist area with revenue of IDR 200 million per month. The owner is convinced the business is highly profitable, because prices per treatment are high and the spa looks busy during the holiday season.

Let us break it down. Wages for therapists and staff, because the spa is labour-intensive, take 45% of revenue. Rent, because it is in a premium tourist location, is 20%, already above the safe threshold. Utilities, laundry and consumables are 12%. Marketing and platform commissions, because many customers come through apps, are 13%. Administration and other costs are 6%.

Total costs already come to 45% + 20% + 12% + 13% + 6% = 96%. That leaves 4%.

And that is in a busy month. The problem appears in the low season. When tourist flows drop, revenue can fall by 30% or more, but rent, fixed wages and basic utilities do not fall with it. In quiet months, the spa can make a loss, and low-season losses eat away at the thin profits of the high season.

What is striking is that the owner felt highly profitable the whole time, because they were looking at the high price per treatment and the crowds of the holiday season. They did not realise that uneven utilisation across the year, rent of 20% that was too heavy, and dependence on commission-based platforms had pushed the margin to a fragile point.

Had the owner raised utilisation in the low season (through the local market or special packages), negotiated rent down to a more reasonable level, or reduced dependence on commission-based channels, those few percentage points could have made the difference between a fragile business and a genuinely healthy one.

How to read these numbers for your spa

A few practical principles:

Separate margin per treatment from business margin. A high price per session creates an illusion. What determines the health of the business is the net margin after all costs, including therapist wages, rent and less-than-full utilisation.

Treat utilisation as your main metric. Monitor how full your treatment rooms are over time, not just at peak periods. Empty beds in the low season are fixed costs that evaporate. Raising utilisation often has more impact than cutting costs.

Do not depend on a single customer segment. The lesson from even a large operator: dependence on one type of tourist makes margins fragile. Build a customer mix, including the local market and repeat customers who are not seasonal.

Keep rent at a reasonable level. Rent is one of the heaviest and least flexible line items in the spa business. A premium location that demands high rent is not necessarily more profitable once the numbers are run.

Count your own salary as a cost. If you work full time in your spa, your time has value. A margin that looks healthy but ignores the owner's salary is an illusory margin.

Plan expansion carefully. Opening a new branch squeezes margins in the short term. Make sure your existing branches are healthy and mature before taking on new fixed costs.

Closing

The spa business holds the same paradox as many businesses with high per-unit margins: an expensive price per treatment creates the impression of a very profitable business, while the actual net margin is much thinner and more fragile. Real data confirms this. The largest spa operator in the region, with full scale and efficiency, posted a net profit margin of only around 11%, and that figure can fall sharply when customer flows change.

For that reason, the 40% figure should not automatically be taken as the ideal net margin for a spa business. The Siam Wellness data shows that a net margin of around 11% can occur even at a large-scale operator, and whether a margin is healthy depends heavily on cost structure, treatment room utilisation and customer patterns. For a business that counts all its costs honestly, a margin in the low teens is already considered healthy, and even that requires disciplined management of three things: labour, rent and, most decisive of all, treatment room utilisation.

The good news is that all of this can be calculated and managed. What is needed is not a higher price per treatment, but a complete understanding of your business's cost structure and revenue patterns.

A healthy business is not only one that makes a profit, but one that understands its numbers, controls its risks, and moves in a clear direction.
